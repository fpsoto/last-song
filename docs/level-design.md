# LAST SONG - Level design

A four-level campaign that walks through the evolution of rock, from the 1950s to the 1980s. The current 1984 stage ("THE LAST BAR") is the proof of concept and the technical base of Level 4.

The story stays simple and ambient. The identity of each decade comes from scenery, enemies, music, atmosphere and bosses, not from long cutscenes. The protagonist is Jack Ryder, a frustrated musician who fights his way through the eras.

Core pillar: **branching routes**. A level can split into different musical currents, each with its own scenery, enemies and boss, which gives real reasons to replay.

## Cross-cutting rules

1. **Music follows the era.** Each level plays music of its decade, and each route plays music of its style. It uses the existing `era.music` / `bossMusic` data in `js/levels.js`.
2. **Regular NPCs share one base body.** Only the outfit and the skin change with the era. Bosses are the exception (see rule 3). Tracked in #32.
3. **Bosses are unique.** Each boss has its own look, behaviour and a signature mechanic that no other boss uses. Tracked in #33 and described below.
4. **Everything is generated by code.** No image or audio files, same as the rest of the game.
5. **Modular levels.** Each level and each route must be developed and tested on its own, with dev flags to reach it directly.

## Campaign overview

| Level | Era | Structure | Boss(es) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1950s, birth of rock | Linear, tutorial | Rockabilly gang leader |
| 2 | 1960s, diversification | Shared opening, one fork, two routes | One per route (psychedelic, classic rock) |
| 3 | 1970s, hard rock and early metal | Linear, harder | Rival band leader |
| 4 | 1980s, musical explosion | Heavy Metal start, two forks, four endings | One per ending (Heavy Metal, Glam Metal, Disco, Pop) |

### Level 1 - 1950s: birth of rock
A single linear route that teaches the game without overloading the player.

- Scenery: American streets and neighbourhoods, diners, parking lots, small music venues and rock and roll stages, with the youth culture look of the time.
- Enemies: gang kids, bikers, neighbourhood thugs.
- Boss: a gang leader with a rockabilly look.
- Level design: introduce controls, attacks, combos, the block and enemies progressively, with a gradual difficulty ramp. No forks.

### Level 2 - 1960s: the diversification of rock
A shared opening in a 1960s setting (streets, music clubs, youth spaces), then a physical fork in the scenery between two musical currents. Once a route is chosen it is followed to the end of the level and the routes never reconnect.

- Route A, psychedelic rock: intense colours, psychedelic lighting, surreal and extravagant scenery. Enemies inspired by counterculture, experimental musicians and youth groups. Own music and ambience. Exclusive psychedelic boss.
- Route B, classic rock: urban streets, rock clubs and concert venues, a more traditional and aggressive ambience. Enemies inspired by street bands, rock followers and club bouncers. Own music and ambience. Exclusive classic rock boss.

### Level 3 - 1970s: hard rock and the birth of heavy metal
A single linear route, harder than Level 2, acting as the transition into the explosion of styles in the 1980s.

- Identity: classic hard rock, the first heavy metal, massive concert culture and heavier bands.
- Scenery: big concert stages, factories and industrial zones, run-down urban areas, rock bars and rehearsal rooms, darker and more aggressive lighting.
- Enemies: concert thugs, rival band followers and hard rock gangs. Tougher, more aggressive, with attack patterns of their own.
- Boss: a musician or leader of a rival band with a hard rock and primitive metal look.
- Level design: harder encounters and a heavier, more forceful combat identity.

### Level 4 - 1980s: musical explosion and commercialisation
Built on the current 1984 stage. Heavy Metal is the starting point and the main route, and the other currents appear as alternatives during the run. There are two forks and four possible endings.

```
0%                40%                60%                100%
Heavy Metal -----+---------------------------------------> Heavy Metal boss (ending 1)
(shared start)   |
                 +--> Glam Metal ---------------------------> Glam Metal boss (ending 2)
                 |
                 +--> Disco -------+--------------------------> Disco boss (ending 3)
                                   |
                                   +--> Pop ------------------> Pop boss (ending 4)
```

**Stage 1 - Heavy Metal (0% to 40%).** Dark streets, concert venues, industrial areas, metal band posters and intense lighting. Enemies: metal gangs, concert thugs and rival band followers. At 40% the first fork appears, with three exits:

- Continue Heavy Metal: the remaining 60% in the same scenery, with genre enemies and the Heavy Metal boss. The main route is a complete route, not filler.
- Go to Glam Metal: leave the metal scenery for a glam metal environment, with its own enemies, the remaining 60% and the Glam Metal boss.
- Go to Disco: leave the metal scenery for a completely different environment (see stage 2).

**Stage 2 - Disco (40% to 60%).** Discos, dance floors, neon lights, mirror balls, night venues and commercial spaces. Enemies: club bouncers, security guards and disco gangs, with movement and personality tied to dance culture. At 60% the second fork appears, **only inside Disco**:

- Continue Disco: the remaining 40% with the Disco boss.
- Go to Pop: scenery tied to television, shows and commercial culture, the remaining 40% and the Pop boss.

Glam Metal and Pop are independent alternatives. They do not connect with each other, and Glam Metal is not reachable from Disco.

**The four endings.**

- Heavy Metal: loyalty to heavy rock and its musical identity.
- Disco: abandoning traditional rock for dance and commercial music.
- Glam Metal and Pop: two different roads to showbiz and the music industry.

Each route has its own boss, final scenery and outcome.

## Branching rules

- **A. Independent paths.** Once the player enters a branch they stay on it until the end of the level, unless a new explicit fork appears.
- **B. The main route is a complete route.** It has its own content, challenges and boss, it is never filler.
- **C. Physical forks.** No path-selection UI or menus. Choices appear inside the scenery as streets, doors, passages, venues and visual cues.
- **D. Exclusive scenery.** Each route substantially changes the atmosphere, visual composition and decoration.
- **E. Exclusive enemies.** Each musical current has enemies that reinforce its theme.
- **F. Exclusive bosses.** Each route ends with a different boss with its own personality, look and combat patterns.
- **G. Balanced length.** Parallel routes have an equivalent distance from the decision point to the final boss. Difficulty does not have to be identical: it can vary through enemy count, toughness, aggressiveness, waves and encounter complexity.

Forks must never be reduced to visual changes. Each route is a different playable experience.

## Replayability

The incentive to replay comes from alternative scenery, new enemy groups, different bosses, different musical identities and alternative endings. No complex narrative is needed to justify the routes: the change of scenery, music and enemies is enough to show a new musical current. Level select and discovered endings are tracked in #31.

## Regular enemies: one body, many eras

Regular NPCs use the same base body in every level. Only outfit, skin and accessories change with the era, so adding a new decade means adding a wardrobe, not new drawing code (#32). Enemy types keep their gameplay differences (health, damage, speed) and must stay readable in combat through outfit and accessories.

**How it works.** `sprites.js` has one `BODY` (proportions and rig) and a `WARDROBES` table (skin, hair, clothes, accessories) with an entry per era; a level segment picks one with `wardrobe: 'id'` (default `metal`). Today: `metal`, `glam`, `disco`. Adding an era means adding data, not drawing code; the only limit is the set of hair styles and accessories `drawBody` already knows.

**How the types stay readable on the same body.** By outfit, not by proportions: the roadie (big, slow) wears dark, chunky clothes (`bulk: 2`) with a beard or shades, the punk (fast) has loud hair and bright accents, and the groupie (light) has a slim cut (`bulk: -2`), big hair and a handbag. Every wardrobe dresses all three types the same way. Hit boxes, animations and timings do not depend on the look. Jack and the bosses keep their own LOOKS, so bosses can have their own proportions and props.

## Bosses

Every boss is unique in look and behaviour, and each one has a signature mechanic that no other boss uses. A 1950s rockabilly boss must not feel like a 1980s heavy metal boss.

**Normal combat always takes health off the boss.** Basic attacks, combos, specials, dodges and the block all reduce the health bar. The signature mechanic complements this: it decides how the opportunity for the final blow appears. It never replaces normal combat.

Catalogue of mechanics (they can be combined when it makes sense, without overloading a fight):

| Mechanic | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Destroy amplifiers | Destroy elements that protect or power up the boss |
| Counter windows | The boss performs special attacks and is left vulnerable for a moment |
| SOLO finisher | Use an opening to deliver a special final attack |
| Rhythm phase | Beat a musical sequence to weaken the boss |
| Musical duel | A musical confrontation between the protagonist and the boss |
| Stage hazards | The environment adds elements that change the fight and create openings |

How the player learns it, without long tutorials or interruptions:

- A short boss intro card with the name, an image, the musical identity and a line that hints at the mechanic, for example "While his amps keep roaring, nobody can stop him".
- Visual and audio cues during the fight: a glowing amp can be destroyed, an exhausted animation means he is vulnerable, a wind-up pose announces a special attack, a signal starts a rhythm phase, a specific animation shows he is ready for the finisher.

Boss design rules:

1. Every boss has its own special mechanic.
2. Normal combat always contributes to reducing his health.
3. The mechanic must offer a vulnerability or finisher opportunity.
4. Each boss communicates visually how his mechanic works.
5. Intro cards give hints, they do not explain the whole fight.
6. Bosses do not have to share mechanics, and they must differ visually and in behaviour.
7. Difficulty increases progressively through the campaign.
8. Mechanics are implemented modularly so new bosses are easy to add.

## Combat additions

- **Block (#13).** A simple, intuitive defensive block, balanced against attacks, combos and dodges, without complicating the combat system. Rules, all tuned in the `BLOCK` table of `js/player.js`:
  - **Input:** hold `I` or `Shift`, or the BLOCK touch button. Only on the ground, only against blows from the front: Jack can turn but not walk while guarding.
  - **Light blows** (not `heavy`, no `knockdown`): 20% of the damage gets through, no flinch, a small slide back.
  - **Heavy blows** (the roadie's haymaker, boss attacks, the boss shockwave): 50% of the damage gets through and Jack staggers briefly out of the guard, but is never knocked down. Heavy attacks are never fully absorbed.
  - **Guard meter (60):** a blocked blow costs 1.4x its damage if light and 2.4x if heavy, so a roadie haymaker can be blocked twice and the boss swing once. A blow the guard cannot pay for breaks it: it lands in full and the block is locked for 1 s. The meter refills (22/s, starting 0.6 s after the last blocked blow) only while Jack is not holding a usable guard, and the block cannot be raised with less than 8 left, so turtling in a crowd wears out fast and fights stay about attack and movement (#10).
  - **Combo and SOLO:** raising the guard ends the punch chain, blocking never charges the SOLO meter, and a punch, kick, jump or the SOLO cancels the block.
  - **Feedback:** a raised-arms pose, a violet ping with a `BLOCK` tag and a knock sound, a guard bar over Jack's head (red while broken), and `GUARD BREAK!` with its own sound.

## Introduction and main menu

- **Splash (#17).** The developer logo (The Dr Juego Studio, redrawn by code) and the LAST SONG logo, 2 to 3 s each, on black or an old amplifier texture, with a short guitar and amp sound.
- **Intro (#18).** 8 to 12 s, no dialogue: out-of-tune guitar on black, an amp switching on, a distant crowd, Jack seen from behind holding his guitar, the title.
- **Main menu (#19).** An empty rock stage with a microphone, amps and guitars in red, violet and blue lighting, the protagonist resting next to an amp with idle animations, lights flashing to the music. Options: Play, Level select (locked until available), Options, Credits.
- **Play transition (#20).** A drum hit, the lights go out, a short visual transition into Level 1.

Intros stay short and arcade: priority goes to music, atmosphere and visual personality, with brief loading and transitions.

## Implementation priorities

1. Consolidate the mechanics and the branching architecture (block, level graph, Level 4 prototype).
2. Build the main routes and their forks, and make sure each decision leads to the right route.
3. Build the exclusive scenery of each route.
4. Create the themed enemies for each musical current (wardrobes on the shared body).
5. Design the exclusive final bosses and their mechanics.
6. Balance the length and difficulty of the routes.
7. Check that every route can be completed independently.

Order of content: the linear levels first (1 and 3), then Level 2 (one fork), then the rest of Level 4. The campaign is tracked in #16.

## Open points

- The Stage Clear teaser now shows the ending of the route that was finished (Level 4 prototype, #15). The order of the eras in `js/levels.js` (1980 is the only playable one) must still be reconciled with this plan as the other levels land.
- Boss names and signature mechanics are chosen when each boss is designed. The current boss, THE KING OF RIFFS, is the first example (#33).
